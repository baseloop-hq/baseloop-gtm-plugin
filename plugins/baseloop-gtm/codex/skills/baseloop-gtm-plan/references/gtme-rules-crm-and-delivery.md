# GTME rules: CRM and delivery

The shared engineering rules every use-case recipe cites, second half: working a CRM (section 3) and delivering (section 4). Taking the request and building it are in [gtme-rules.md](./gtme-rules.md) (sections 1 and 2); read both before planning any build.

The build skill cannot read this file, so every rule that shapes a lookup, a write, a gate or a delivery goes into the plan as a specific. Cost here is shape only: free, paid per row, paid on success, or free on the org's own key. Read the figure from the action's guide (`get_action_schema`) when it states one, and from the build's one-row test; `creditCostHint` in `list_actions` says only free, paid or variable. The HubSpot actions named here are examples of the shape; for another CRM, read that provider's actions from `list_actions`.

## 3. CRM

**Working from the CRM.** A CRM row is the only source that lies confidently: formatted, full and stale. From contacts, import contact fields only, name, email, title, LinkedIn URL, record id, associated company id, contact history, through the CRM's list or criteria import with a record limit (never typed in with `create_rows`); then look up the company record with that company id (`hubspot_lookup_object`) and take company-level data from there, domain, industry, size, owner, deals, last contacted. Company data on a contact record is a copy, often stale: the company record is shared across contacts and corrected once, so when the two disagree the company record wins. Account status decides before any spend: a customer, or an account with an open deal or contacted within the cutoff, is handed to its owner, not re-engaged, and an owner alone is not a signal because the owner field never expires. Check the current position before spending on the old company; when a person has left, flag the row and let the rep decide: a lead who moved is a different sales conversation.

From companies, the company row is the account. The CRM company check, the create and the company id live on the company table; contacts found or looked up from it pull the id through their account lookup and never create companies. Contacts match back to their account by the company LinkedIn URL first, then the LinkedIn company id, then the domain, then the company name last.

**Leads in a request means contacts.** A CRM's Leads object is a separate, thinly used object most teams never work in; when a user says leads they mean the contacts they call and email, and sometimes the companies behind them. Plan on contacts, and state in the plan which object the recipe reads, so a user who does mean the object can correct it before anything is built. When the user names the Leads object itself, keep it. These tools cannot count Leads records without importing them, so plan on contacts, say so in the plan, and confirm with the user when anything the user said suggests the Leads object.

**Ask what counts as an account to leave alone.** Every company records "in play" differently, so the test is asked, never assumed: which accounts must the recipe not touch, an open deal, an owner assigned, given lifecycle stages, contacted recently and how recently (HubSpot's last-contacted property is `notes_last_contacted`; confirm it with `resolve_action_options`). Read the portal's own values back to the user instead of guessing (the property's options from `resolve_action_options`), because lifecycle stages are custom per portal, a stage named customer is not proof of a customer, and custom stages arrive as numeric ids. This is silent and expensive in both directions: too strict and the recipe hands everything to owners and revives nothing, too loose and a rep re-approaches a live account. Where a person is claimed by whoever touches them first, ask the claim rule the same way: how long the hold lasts, what counts as a touch, whether a colleague's record can be reassigned. Policy, never a number inside a formula.

**Check the CRM twice.** Once before paid work, on whatever the row arrived with, so you never pay to enrich a contact the CRM already has. Once after, on the identifier the chain produced, usually the merged email, because it decides duplicates and did not exist before. Both match on everything identifying a person, combined with OR (`hubspot_lookup_object` filters are OR groups of AND conditions): first name, last name and company; first name, last name and domain; the LinkedIn URL. The second adds the merged email. Never name alone, never email alone: the file's email and the CRM's email for the same person often differ. Found means update, not found means create: gate the create on the lookup's `isNotFound` and the update on the same lookup's `isFound`, give the create every property the update writes, and never create without the second check. Where the destination has a unique key, that key is a precondition of the create: a contact created with no email cannot be found or updated, and the next arrival with one creates the duplicate the check existed to stop. A free check meant to avoid paying for what the system of record holds queries the system of record (the CRM lookup, which is free): a table in the workspace is one list imported once, and every record it lacks reads as absent forever.

**Companies are created from their own table.** A HubSpot company create never dedupes, so a contacts table updates companies but never creates them, or three contacts at one company create three companies. Rows whose company is not in the CRM go to a companies table with auto-dedupe on the domain column (`set_auto_dedupe`, `keepRule: "oldest"`), switched on after a one-row send creates that column and before the full run, are created from there once, and the contacts fetch the id back and associate. A formula cannot see other rows, so dedupe is a table setting, not a column. Keep one create, one update and one note per record: merge several lookup paths into one Effective ID formula first. Dedupe against the destination, not your own copy of it: a workspace table of companies is a snapshot, and when it lags the build creates a duplicate in the live CRM, so the CRM lookup runs first and the owned table carries the key for the create only. A company's website domain and its email domain can differ, and a CRM may associate a contact by the email domain on its own, so check the CRM's own behaviour before relying on the association you set. Two tables creating the same object in one destination send the same property set, or the record one made cannot be found by the other's lookup and the build makes its own duplicates.

**Write back only what changed.** Update by record id with ignore blanks on (`ignoreBlanks: true` on `hubspot_update_object`), so an empty result never erases a value, and send a property only where the CRM's value is empty or wrong, so a run on a live CRM touches only the records that need it. An empty mapped cell is never sent, so an empty cell cannot clear a field; to clear a HubSpot text or dropdown property on purpose, map a fixed empty value with ignore blanks off; number, date, checkbox and phone properties cannot be cleared from a table. A second write that needs the create's returned id (a note on the new record, an association) is gated on that id being present, because every failed create drops the second write with it in silence; a property write on the same record is folded into the create, never a second update gated on the id existing, which writes each new record twice. One property, one writer: when two steps can write the same field their order is undefined, so one column owns the fact and exactly one action writes it. A note never prints an empty value, so branch on whether the value exists: a wrong sentence on a live record cannot be edited from the table. A column whose only job is to correct what an earlier column wrote is a design review, not a fix: the earlier column is the bug. An email the CRM refuses as taken is a duplicate found, not an error: HubSpot's create reports `Already exists` with the existing record's id (`duplicate: true` in its `fullValue`) and writes nothing to it, so show that id on the row as its own word and leave the merge to the rep. A row a person has to act on gets a task with an owner and a due date (`hubspot_create_engagement` with the Task type), unless the plan names a view as the queue.

**The sample before a CRM write is a data review, not a smoke test.** Before the full run of any write onto a live CRM, show the sample rows, ten or every eligible row when fewer, with each value to write next to the CRM's current value and the decision word, say how many records change and which properties, and wait for the user. Approval of the plan is not approval of the data: on the write depth the ladder exists so the user reads the values, and a write that goes to the full run on plan approval alone is a defect. The plan names this review as its own step before the full run.

**A signal's identity is the event, not the account.** The key deciding "have we told them this already" carries what happened and when, never only who: a key on the company alone turns the second real signal into a repeat forever, and the alert stops firing. Take the identifier the source supplies before deriving one. A repeat never raises a second alert: the key drops it (auto-dedupe on the event or signal id column of the table the signals land in, never on a table its own import refreshes). Repeats are not counted, because the dedupe deletes them: a user who asks to track them is told this build cannot count them reliably.

**Delivery is recorded where the next run reads it, and a verdict is not a delivery record.** Re-running the judgment overwrites the answer an alert was sent on, so what was sent, to whom and when is its own column or a CRM note, written at send time and never recomputed. A property holds a state and may be overwritten; an event goes in a dated note, or the second erases the first. A property written back as a fixed value is a queue: say what sets it and what clears it before it is built, or the record is processed once and never again, or forever. A status only moves forward: Replied outranks Bounced outranks Started outranks Added, in a formula that writes only when the new rank beats the current one, so an out-of-order event cannot walk a record backwards.

**A source that stops looks exactly like a source with nothing to report.** Silence is the normal answer for a signal, so a dead feed goes unnoticed for weeks. Every scheduled or webhook source is checked for that, never with a formula (it sees one row and recomputes only when that row changes): by the newest row's Created At against the interval (a view sorted on Created At), and a scheduled source also through its runs (`list_runs` shows a failed fire; `get_run_status` shows the rows a fire created in its `sourceImportSummary` where the import records them; an RSS pull records the items it read, the rows it created and the newest item's date).

**A create carries a key that stops a second one.** A note, a task or a record created twice cannot be undone from the table, and a repaired row re-runs; with `autoUpdateDependents` on, any change to a column the note reads re-runs it with skip-filled-cells off and posts again. Before any create, read what the destination already holds for this key, the note link, the call id, the meeting date, and gate the create on it; a re-run then updates the row and writes nothing twice. An add to a sequencer campaign is a create: the person plus the campaign is the key, the sequencer's own duplicate check stays strict (Allow Duplicate Leads Across Campaigns, `allowDuplicate`, off on lemlist and Smartlead; Skip if in Any Campaign, `skipIfInWorkspace`, on for Instantly), and the enrolment is a record in the CRM, not a property on the contact, because a property holds one campaign and a record holds the history. A failed add becomes a word on the row and a task for a person, never a flag left set at the source that fires again on its own. Whether a record was created or found is written into its own column at the moment of the create, because the lookup that answered it re-runs later and erases the answer ([pitfalls.md](./pitfalls.md) "A note, task or campaign add created twice cannot be undone").

**On a recurring write-back, read the record first and write only the difference.** Read the destination's current values into columns, compute the intended values, and let the difference be the only gate on the write: it makes the run safe to repeat, keeps a live CRM quiet, and turns "have we already told them this" into a comparison instead of a memory the agent is asked to keep. A history table keyed on the source row is a mirror of the current state; a history is keyed on the run. The same live read gates the paid step, not only the write: on a schedule the import's copy is up to one interval old and a person may have filled the field since, so the condition on an expensive lookup reads the destination's current value. A date saying when work happened is written by the step that did the work, never by a formula that recomputes, or the record's "enriched on" field records the last recalculation.

**A resolution is worth keeping, and what enters the memory needs a higher bar than what passes one write.** When a build works out which record an arrival belongs to, the answer is expensive and the same arrival comes back, so keep it in a memory table, one row per source and identifier, holding the destination's id, how it was decided, when, and the confidence; every later arrival checks it first for free (`lookup_single_record`). A match good enough to act on once is not good enough to be believed forever, so the confidence that writes into memory is stricter than the one that passes the row. When a memory table gains a second key column, every consumer moves to the merged key in the same change, or the older ones go quiet on exactly the rows the new key was added for.

**A soft no is an asset, a hard no is a boundary.** Not now, we just signed with someone else, no budget this year, we are doing it in house, ask me in Q3: every one is a reason to come back, because the condition expires. Remove me, never contact me again, we are not interested: never, and the row has to say so plainly so nobody re-approaches them by accident. Wherever outcomes are mapped onto fewer words, the mapping never merges the two: those are opposite instructions, and one wrong bucket is a compliance problem, not a data-quality one. A distinction the list makes is only real where the destinations make it too, so every word worth separating needs its own visible consequence: audit the routes, not the list. A flag that stops nothing is worse than no flag, because "a human must confirm" next to writes that fire anyway teaches the reader that warnings are decorative.

A list a machine writes into needs the same do-not-contact test as a message a person sends, because the row still reaches the person through whatever the list is wired to: ask what must never be in it, and carry a word that blocks the add. Every gate that would sit in front of a send sits in front of the add, the campaign end date, the CRM state of the account, the duplicate key, the blocklist, and in front of every add in the table: a second channel added later inherits none of the first channel's gates, so count the gates per channel. The handoff inherits every gate the record write below it has, or people reach the outside world that the system of record never heard of. A suppression word belongs to a channel, not to a person: the same person can be off limits on one and open on the other. A filter meaning "we cannot reach this person" belongs in the same place as one meaning "we may not contact this person", because both stop the send, and splitting them, one in a field and one in a view, leaves the second to whoever is at the keyboard. The same exclusion written as prompt text, as a formula and as a run condition drifts into three different memberships and only the one nearest the send decides anything, so the list lives in one table joined on an identifier.

**Signal history comes from the delivery record.** A repeated alert wastes the rep's attention, so the history must record actual delivery. Save every delivered signal where the next run reads it, its own column or a CRM note; a note uses a supplied CRM id when present and otherwise resolves the record first. Read that record before researching new signals, and save the new alert to the CRM before any Slack or email notification. Never substitute typed test history for real delivery records.

## 4. Deliver

**Keep the table honest.** Only the columns the input brought plus what the recipe needs, and none for fields the input never had. Plain names a user understands: "Merged Domain", "Work email", "Already in the CRM? (by email or name)". One view, system columns hidden (`update_view_fields` with `visible: false`). No merge column for a field only one source can fill: a merge of one value is a copy. A helper column says what happens to the row, not Yes or No to a question the user never asked: "Company creation: already in the CRM" reads, "First contact at this company? Yes" does not.

A mapping between an internal id and a human name, owners, stages, countries, lives in one place, read from the destination that holds it (`resolve_action_options` returns the CRM's own ids): copies drift, and a placeholder left in one fails every create it feeds. Where the destination has a fixed list, the mapping is complete or the value is not sent, and a name mapping to nothing is an error word that stops the row, never an empty that skips in silence. The same holds for a judgment: a classification several recipes share is one step with one prompt, reused, because a copy per table drifts on model, on guards and eventually on meaning. Two columns whose names differ only in case are one column to every reader. The column's name is part of the prompt and can outweigh it, so name the column for exactly the value the prompt asks for. Read an ordered helper formula from the bottom up before shipping it: a rule testing a column the excluded rows never receive can never fire, and the rows it was written for take the wrong word in silence. A probe asking the destination what it calls a field answers once: the answer becomes the config and the probe is deleted, or every later run carries columns nobody can read.

**Format for the destination.** A model mapping into a destination's fixed list is handed the whole list, or it does not write: a subset is a silent rewrite, every value outside it replaced by the nearest one inside, with a success status. A catch-all is a word for a person, never a value for a machine. A cleaner is safe only where its rule cannot destroy a legitimate spelling, so it carries its exception list before it runs. Text written for one destination arrives broken in another, and a wall of text is a delivery failure even when the write succeeded: check how the destination renders before the write and convert in a formula, because some CRMs render a note as HTML and collapse plain line breaks, chat tools take their own markup, and email stays a subject and a body. Never assume a destination behaves like the last one you verified. Where the destination matches records on a fixed set of keys, an ad platform or a mail house, that key list is the spec: it decides which columns exist, which gaps are worth paying to fill, and what the readiness word tests, so write it down with the user before building.

**Where the request names records, the table is the deliverable.** A pipeline, a segment, a saved list, a stage: the work is a table carrying one row per record with the analysis columns on it, and the build report previews it: the top few rows and the play to run. A ranked list typed into a message cannot be filtered, corrected, re-run or handed to a rep, and the next record to arrive is not in it. Tell me and build it are one request wherever the subject is a set of records, so plan the build, unless the user says not to build: then answer for the records named and offer the table once.

**Deliver the specifics next to the verdict.** One word filters a list; it does not open a conversation. The row carries both: the word, and what happened in the person's own wording with a date on it, both out of the same agent call as separate outputs, never a second call. When the work ends in a CRM task, the specifics go in the task body, because that is where the rep reads them. One message per exception is a report in the wrong place: when the exception rate reaches a third of the table the exception is the deliverable, so it gets a column, a view and one summary per run.

**Nothing found is an answer.** An empty answer spelled as a word passes every emptiness filter: a step told to return "Not found" reads as found in every status count and every not-empty gate, so either return empty or let one named helper column own the phrase, and spell the empty value once while reading both spellings downstream, because models add a full stop ([pitfalls.md](./pitfalls.md) "Sentinel words pass every not-empty gate"). State the empty case once, as the only instruction: "return something if anything credible exists" next to "leave empty if nothing is findable" is one instruction, and the model obeys the first. A miss and a failure are different states, and a fallback gated on the miss alone (`isNotFound` without `hasError`) silently abandons the failures. A derived label never falls back to a weaker input: let the input stay empty and the label stay empty with it. Facts degrade honestly and judgments do not, so a judgment output is gated on at least one fact having come back.

Most rows, most weeks, there is nothing. That result gets its own word on the row, never blended with a row that failed or has not run. A recipe returning something every time is not working, it is stretching: silence is what makes the alerts that do fire worth reading. A signal at a company nobody owns still lands somewhere named, a list a person works or a default owner, decided before the build, because gating delivery on an owner silently loses every unowned account. On a job running over a whole object the word per row is not the deliverable: the owner's question is coverage, so the build ends in a count per field, filled before, filled now, still empty, failed. Where the work is per account and the output is per person, the account carries its own word: how many seats are filled and which are missing by name. A verdict gating the rest of the chain carries a third word for the rows it never judged, because an empty verdict reads as a skip and stops every step behind it in silence; that word is usually the largest bucket and gets its own exit and its own count. A message saying the work is done is gated on the action that changed the destination, never on the bookkeeping step after it, because a step that cannot fail turns every failure into an announced success.

**The user reads one word.** The last column is Result, one word per row: Done, Incomplete, Unreachable, Pending, or the equivalent for the use case. One word filters. Pending comes from a helper column that always has a value, never from an action column being empty, because an action that has not run and one that found nothing both look empty to a formula: the helper asks "did the step run?" before "did it find anything?". Derive the not-run state from the last step that could have produced the answer, not the first, or the Result says No email on rows where the email step never ran. A row the first free gate excluded is a decided row, not an empty one, and it gets its word; without it the exclusion rate is unmeasurable and a broken classifier looks like a quiet week.

**Offer the next depth; never assume it.** When a depth is built and verified, the plan or the build report ends with one closing line naming the next depth, at most three options, deepest first. Each names the table, the records and the work in full, so it reads as a new request rather than a continuation of a plan that is already finished. On no, that leg is never created.

**The boundary.** Baseloop delivers data. It does not send, call, book, or create pipeline. Outreach tools send, reps call, CRMs remain the working system. Coverage depends on the providers connected: say "verified", never "guaranteed".
